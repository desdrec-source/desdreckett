20 September 2026 · Discovery calls

Views are not the metric. Enquiries are.

The platform scoreboard is distribution. The practice scoreboard is inbound that already wants the work.

YouTube will show you views. Average view duration. Click-through. Subscribers.

Those numbers tell you whether the platform is distributing the video. They don't tell you whether a buyer is about to book.

A video can do a few hundred views and still send the right two conversations. Another can do tens of thousands and recruit an audience that will never buy the work. Both can look like growth if you only watch the creator scoreboard.

I'm going to be honest with you. This is where a lot of capable people waste a year.

Two scoreboards

There's the platform scoreboard. Views. Watch time. Subs. Nice graphs. Easy to screenshot.

There's the practice scoreboard. Enquiries that mention a video. Calls that arrive already knowing the shape of the offer. Hours spent this month versus the six-hour cap.

Only one of those pays the firm.

You can improve the first scoreboard by making content for a wider room. Broader titles. Softer promises. A bit of theatre. The second scoreboard usually gets worse when you do that. You get strangers. You lose the person who was about to hire you.

Can you see how this happens? You post something useful to a buyer. It does all right. Then you post something that travels in the peer group. It does better. So you make more of the second kind. Six months later you have a channel and an empty Tuesday.

The content worked. On the wrong scoreboard.

What to count instead

Count the messages that mention a video, a question, or "I've been watching."

Count the calls that start halfway through the explanation.

Count which titles produced those conversations. Not which titles got the spike.

Count the hours. If the hours crept past six and the enquiries didn't move, the process is bloated. Cut the process.

If you want a platform number at all, keep it in the basement. Don't let it set the week. I look at views the way I look at weather. Useful to notice. Useless as a plan.

Monetisation is a side door

SME threads still ask when to switch on AdSense. Fair question if the channel is the business. For a practice, ad revenue is a rounding error next to one retained client.

Turn it on if you qualify. There's no prize for leaving money on the table. Don't build the channel around it. The moment you start choosing topics because they "monetise better," you've left the buyer and joined a different game.

Same with subscriber milestones. One thousand subscribers unlocks tools. It doesn't unlock a pipeline. I've seen quiet channels send work and loud channels send compliments. I know which one I'd take.

Agencies and the wrong dashboard

If you hire help, watch the dashboard they put in the monthly deck.

If it's views, subs, and posting volume, they're building a creator channel on your time. If it's which search questions you now own, and which conversations arrived pre-sold, they're in the right job.

Keep the Google account in the firm's name. Anyone who holds the channel under their own login is a risk. Anyone who guarantees a subscriber count by a date doesn't control the thing they're selling.

Ask what question they'd have you answer first. If they start with posting frequency and kit, smile and leave.

A week of looking at the right number

Pick a month. Write down every inbound conversation. Next to each one, write how they found you. Referral. LinkedIn. Event. Video. "I've been watching."

You don't need a fancy CRM for this. A note will do.

If video never appears, you have a findability problem or a topic problem. Usually topic. The videos are for peers, or they don't match the sentence buyers use.

If video appears and the calls are still messy, the video is attracting interest without pre-selling the work. Tighten the answer. Say who it's for. Say who it's not for. Soft reach is not the same as a qualified enquiry.

Does that make sense?

Why views feel like safety

Views are public. Enquiries are private. That's why people cling to the graph.

You can show a view count in a room. You can tell yourself the week mattered. An enquiry takes longer to arrive and it asks something of you. A call. A proposal. The possibility of a no.

So the graph becomes a comfort object. I'm not mocking that. I've watched the number too. Then I ask what happened in the inbox. If the inbox is quiet, the graph was entertainment.

There's a version of this that looks sophisticated. Dashboards. Retention curves. Thumbnail tests. All useful later. Fatal early, if they replace the question "did anyone who should have hired me, find me?"

A note on small numbers

A few hundred views on a sharp B2B question can be a full room. That search isn't supposed to look like a cooking channel.

If you compare your graph to a creator you like, you'll feel behind. You're not behind. You're in a different market. Stop using their scoreboard.

Write the number of enquiries this month on a piece of paper. Put it above the YouTube analytics tab. That sounds crude. It works. The tab will still be there. It just won't run the week.

The metric that matters is inbound from people who already want the work. That's the whole engine.

Views can come with that. They can also come instead of that. Don't confuse the two.

Community if you want the method. A call if you want it built. No rush at all.

I'd rather you ran a quiet channel that sends work than a busy one that sends applause. Applause is lovely. It doesn't pay the practice.
