20 September 2026 · One video a week

How often should a consultant post on YouTube?

Daily is a different job. The number that holds in a practice is one useful video a week.

Daily is a different job.

Creator forums will tell you to post more. Sometimes they'll tell you to post every day. Feed Shorts in the morning so the algorithm "knows you're serious." Batch twelve videos on a Sunday. Never break the chain.

That advice assumes your calendar is the channel. In a practice, the calendar is client work. A cadence that only survives a quiet January is not a cadence.

So the real question isn't "how often does YouTube prefer?" It's "what is the smallest rhythm a busy consultant can hold for a year?"

The number that holds

One video a week.

About six hours a month across script, film, edit, and publish.

That's enough to build a search estate without turning you into a production company. I'll be honest with you. It's not the number that maximises raw output. It's the number a practice can still respect when three clients land in the same fortnight.

Consistency beats a sprint. You already know this from the rest of the work. Eight videos in March, silence until September, then a new strategy deck. That's the failed version. It shows up in every industry. Video is not special.

Can you see why daily falls apart? Daily needs a room in the week that doesn't exist. Then people feel behind. Then they stop. Then they decide YouTube "doesn't work for them." What failed was the cadence, not the platform.

What "one a week" is not

It's not a Short every weekday plus a long video plus a live plus a newsletter recap of the live.

It's one useful long answer to one question.

If a Short helps point at that answer, fine. It isn't the spine. Shorts are good at discovery. They're weak at pre-selling a piece of work that costs real money. The buyer about to hire a consultant needs more than a hook. They need to hear you think.

Length follows the question. Three minutes if that's the honest answer. Twelve if they need the working. Pad neither. Padding is how you get a video that looks finished and feels empty.

And please don't add a second platform until the weekly slot survives a dirty week. LinkedIn can wait. TikTok can wait. The search result doesn't care that you were also "showing up" somewhere else.

When the week explodes

It will. Delivery weeks eat the slot. That's normal.

Plan for it. Keep a list of CAT Moments so you're never inventing a topic at nine on a Thursday. Batch two questions on a calmer day. Film both. Publish one. Hold one.

If you miss a week, publish the next one. Don't write a guilt post about consistency. The search result doesn't care about your apology. Buyers don't need the story of why you were busy. They need the answer they came for.

And if you miss three weeks in a row, the process is too heavy. Something in script, kit, or editing has swollen. Cut the process before you cut the promise. A plain video that ships beats a beautiful one that lives on a hard drive.

How the six hours actually get used

People hear "six hours a month" and think I mean six hours of filming. I don't.

A workable split looks like this:

  • An hour to pick the question and shape the title.
  • An hour to speak the answer and tidy the opening.
  • A couple of hours to cut the waffle and put the link where a buyer can find it.
  • A little time to write the description in their language, not yours.

AI can take the boring bits. Titles to test. A first pass on the description. A rough cut of the transcript. It should not take the judgement. The judgement is which question is actually a CAT Moment, and which one is just something you fancy talking about.

Now, obviously, this is not perfect. Some weeks the edit runs long because you said the same thing four times. That's a talking problem, not a software problem. Answer it once. Stop.

The algorithm question people hide inside this one

When someone asks how often to post, they often mean "how do I please the algorithm?"

You don't please it by being frequent. You please it, if that word even applies, by being useful to the person who clicked. Watch time on the right video beats a spray of Shorts that attract people who will never buy the work.

A weekly search video can keep working in month six. A daily post is dead by Friday. Those are different machines. Don't run the practice on the second one.

You with me on that?

What a week looks like when it's working

Monday or Tuesday, you pick the question from the list. Not from a blank page. From calls.

You speak the answer into a phone or a laptop. You don't write an essay first. If you need notes, three beats: the opening sentence, the mistake people make, and what you do instead.

You cut the false starts. You leave the ums if they sound like you. You write a description that repeats the buyer language, then one next step. Community, a page, a conversation.

That's a week. It should feel almost boring. Boring is how a cadence survives.

If the week starts to include a new tool, a new series idea, and a debate about thumbnails, you've left the job. Come back to the list.

Who should not use weekly

If you're trying to become a full-time creator, weekly is slow. That's a different ambition. I'm not building that.

If your buyers only ever hire from a room you already own, and inbound is a vanity project, you don't need this article. You need to decide whether YouTube is even the job.

If you can't name one question a client has asked in the last month, weekly will just produce twelve vague videos. Fix the conversations first.

If the thing that has you stuck is the hours, say so. The method exists so the hours stay small.

One video a week. About six hours a month. A list of questions buyers already ask. That's the cadence.

The weekly stack is in the free community if you want the longer walkthrough. If you want it installed rather than studied, that's a conversation.

No rush at all. Better a year of weekly than a heroic month and a quiet autumn.
