Is YouTube worth it if you already have a practice?

Not subscriber maths. The evenings. And whether the calendar still looks the same after you give them up.

When someone asks if YouTube is worth it, they rarely mean the platform. They mean the evenings.

They already have clients. Their diary fills itself some weeks and looks like a desert the next. They've been told more than once that they "should be doing video." And they can feel the second job forming in the back of their head before they've even opened the camera app.

So let's start there. Not with subscriber maths. With the actual question:

Is this going to steal time from the work that pays, and still leave the calendar looking the same?

That's a fair question. Most YouTube advice is written for people whose product is the channel. Yours isn't. Your product is the work. The channel only matters if it sends you people who already want that work.

What "worth it" actually means in a firm

Worth it is not AdSense. I'll be upfront: for a coach or consultant, ad revenue is a rounding error next to one retained client.

Worth it is not a subscriber milestone, either. A thousand subscribers is a YouTube setting. It is not a business model.

Worth it is a qualified enquiry from someone who has already heard you answer the question they typed when they were stuck. The call that starts with "I've been watching your video on X" is not the same call as the one that starts with "So, what do you do?"

You already know that. You've felt the difference in the room. YouTube is simply the place that conversation can begin before anyone books.

Industry surveys keep saying video helps with leads and sales. Wyzowl's 2026 report put lead generation at 85% of video marketers and sales lift at 83%. Treat that as weather, not a promise about your next quarter. The useful part is the pattern: buyers prefer to learn a complex offer on video before they spend an hour with you. That has been true for years. It is still true.

When it is not worth it

I won't pretend YouTube is always the right move. Skip it, or pause it, if any of these are true:

  • You'll only publish when the week is quiet. Quiet weeks are a myth in a practice. If the plan depends on a gap appearing, the plan is already dead.
  • You want the channel to look exciting rather than useful. Excitement is a creator's problem. Usefulness is a buyer's.
  • You're measuring the week by views from other coaches. That's a peer channel. It can feel busy while the diary stays empty.
  • You're about to hire an agency to "make you a creator" on a retainer that looks like a junior salary. That's a different product. It isn't this.

If that's you this month, fine. Do the work. Come back when the question is inbound, not identity.

The version that fits a practice

Here's the version I install.

One search question. One video a week. About six hours a month to script, film, edit, and publish. That's the whole cadence. Everything else is a simple process, so the hours don't creep.

You're not building an audience for its own sake. You're building a search estate: a set of videos that sit on the questions your best clients already ask. When the next buyer types the same thing at ten at night, they find you. Not a directory. Not a peer. Not an LLM with no name attached.

That's the whole argument.

LinkedIn will bury the same post by Thursday. A search video can still be pre-selling in six months. That's why YouTube is the engine and the feed is just a surface.

A better test than "is it worth it?"

Write down the last three clients who paid properly. Not the easy ones. The ones you'd take again.

Now write the sentence they used before they hired you. The messy one. The one they said on the first call while still trying to be polite about how stuck they were.

That sentence is your first video.

If you can't name the sentence, YouTube isn't the problem. Positioning is. Fix that first. A camera won't save a fuzzy offer.

If you can name it, you already have more strategy than most content calendars. You don't need twelve pillars and a colour-coded grid. You need that sentence as a title, answered properly, in the language they used.

Then you do it again next week with the next sentence.

What the first month is for

The first month isn't for going viral. It's for proving the hours can stay small, and that you can finish a video a buyer would actually search.

  1. Week one: pick the sentence. Title it in their words.
  2. Week two: film it the way you'd explain it on a call. Not a keynote. A call.
  3. Week three: publish, then write the next sentence from a real conversation.
  4. Week four: look at who commented or got in touch. Peers saying "so true", or a specific person describing their situation?

That last check is the only audit that matters this early. Views will lie to you. Language in a message won't.

Time, honestly

Six hours a month is the cap I work to. Not because it's magic. Because a practice can hold it.

If you spend twelve, something in the process has swollen. Usually editing. Sometimes the script. Sometimes the belief that the video has to look like someone else's channel.

It will feel awkward at first. First videos do. That's not a sign to wait. It's the cheap end of the learning curve.

Stick to the hour cap. Don't add a second platform until the first cadence holds through a busy delivery week. If it only works in January, it doesn't work.

YouTube is worth it when you treat it as inbound infrastructure, not a personality project.

Either way is fine. I'd rather you stayed in the work than built a channel that eats the work.
